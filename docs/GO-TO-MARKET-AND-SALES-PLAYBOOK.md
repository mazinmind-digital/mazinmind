# MazinMind Digital Go-to-Market & Sales Playbook

**Version:** 1.0 | **Prepared:** 2026-11-04 | **Status:** Working hypothesis, not validated market research

This playbook defines the first customer segments, discovery process, demo flow, outreach standards, and partner strategy. It does not establish market validation, customer results, legal approval, or permission to contact any person. Public business contact information is not blanket consent.

## Executive recommendation

Begin discovery with independent auto repair shops in Greater Boston. Compare them with HVAC/plumbing/home services and salons/spas before locking the niche. The initial offer is a Missed-Inquiry & Lead-Handling Audit, not a promise that an unbuilt phone agent answers calls or books appointments.

MazinMind helps small service businesses capture, organize, and follow up on inquiries using practical automation, CRM workflows, and AI-assisted intake, while keeping humans in control of decisions and exceptions.

## Ideal customer profiles

### A. Independent auto repair and tire shops
- Buyer: owner, general manager, or service manager.
- Likely shape: one to three locations, small front-desk/service-advisor team, recurring inbound calls and appointment requests.
- Pain hypothesis: advisors are busy with customers and repair orders; calls go unanswered during rush periods; web inquiries and voicemails lack clear callback ownership; estimate follow-up is manual.
- Buying triggers: hiring pressure, expansion, seasonal demand, inconsistent follow-up, missed-call volume, or new shop-management software.
- Value proposition: map missed inquiry points, centralize web leads, create callback tasks, and design a safe intake workflow with human escalation.
- Objections: existing shop system; customer preference for a person; concern about incorrect repair/pricing information; limited implementation time.
- Qualification: reachable owner sponsor, recurring inquiry volume, measurable response problem, willingness to baseline and pilot, and a safe integration path.
- Disqualifiers: expectation of autonomous diagnosis/quoting or no human owner for exceptions.

### B. HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and home services
- Buyer: owner, operations manager, dispatcher, or office manager.
- Pain hypothesis: technicians are on jobs while calls arrive; urgent and routine inquiries compete; callbacks lack ownership; after-hours requests wait.
- Buying triggers: seasonal surges, emergency-call burden, growth, staff turnover, missed estimates.
- Value proposition: structured intake for service type, location, urgency, callback preference, and escalation.
- Objections: safety, dispatch integration, availability accuracy, and human preference.
- Qualification: documented service territory/hours, emergency escalation rules, dispatcher owner, and a measurable missed-response baseline.
- Disqualifiers: expectation that AI dispatches emergency crews or guarantees appointments without calendar integration.

### C. Salons, spas, and appointment-led services
- Buyer: owner, studio manager, or front-desk lead.
- Pain hypothesis: staff cannot answer every call while serving customers; repetitive questions interrupt work; rescheduling and cancellation follow-up is inconsistent.
- Value proposition: organize common questions and appointment requests, capture callback details, and route changes to staff or an approved booking system.
- Qualification: repetitive intake, approved FAQs, known booking system, and human owner for exceptions.
- Disqualifiers: no approved booking integration or expectation of changing bookings without checking real availability.

## 10-minute discovery script

1. How do customers usually contact you: phone, website, text, walk-in, or referrals?
2. When are inquiries hardest to answer?
3. What happens when a call is missed or a web form arrives after hours?
4. Who owns the callback, and how do you know it happened?
5. Which inquiry types are repetitive, and which always need a person?
6. What systems hold customer, appointment, and job information today?
7. What is the consequence of delayed response? Can you estimate volume rather than guess revenue?
8. What would you never allow an automated system to do?
9. What would a low-risk 30-day test need to demonstrate?
10. Who approves a pilot, data access, and recurring spend?

Qualification checklist:
- [ ] Owner/authorized sponsor identified.
- [ ] Inquiry sources and approximate weekly volume recorded.
- [ ] Recent missed-response example described.
- [ ] Existing CRM/shop/calendar tools identified.
- [ ] Human escalation owner and hours identified.
- [ ] Prohibited actions and sensitive workflows documented.
- [ ] Baseline metric and pilot success threshold agreed.
- [ ] Data access, privacy, consent, and security requirements reviewed.
- [ ] Next step and decision date recorded.

## Demo runbook

Use fictional data only. Reset sample leads before the demo and clearly label simulated behavior.

1. Set expectations: explain which actions are simulated and which integrations are live.
2. Show a common service request and collect only necessary details.
3. Show qualification: service type, urgency, callback preference, and human handoff.
4. Show lead visibility and current storage limitations.
5. Map proposed CRM handoff, callback task, and consent-aware follow-up as a design unless the integration is live and tested.
6. Close by asking what is missing, agreeing one measurable problem, and scheduling technical discovery.

Never claim a real call was answered, text/email sent, appointment booked, CRM updated, or emergency dispatched unless the action was demonstrated against the live system and verified.

## Objection handling

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| We already have a CRM/shop system. | That may be the right system. First see whether inquiry capture and callback ownership work inside it before proposing another tool. |
| Customers want a human. | Agreed for many situations. Automation should collect context and route exceptions, not trap customers. |
| AI will quote the wrong price. | Pricing stays with approved rules or a human. The demo does not authorize autonomous estimates. |
| We cannot change our workflow. | Do not force a platform change. A pilot should fit the existing process and include rollback. |
| What results can you guarantee? | None without a measured baseline and pilot. Agree metrics and report actual results, not promises. |
| How is customer data handled? | Document data flow, access, retention, deletion, and vendor terms before real data is used. Otherwise use synthetic data only. |

## Partnership strategy

Prioritize introductions and co-education before paid referral arrangements. No compensation, exclusivity, data sharing, or contract is authorized by this document.

| Partner category | Rationale | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Auto repair associations/shop networks | Owner/operator access | Identify local chapter and ask about educational member resources |
| Shop-management consultants | Understand existing shop systems | Request technical discovery |
| Local web/SEO agencies | Clients may need lead-response workflows | Offer joint workflow review with clear boundaries |
| Managed IT providers | Trusted business relationships | Compare referral fit; no client data sharing |
| HVAC/plumbing trade groups | Concentrated service-business audience | Ask about practical missed-inquiry workshop |
| Bookkeepers/accountants | Hear operational pain from owners | Share an educational checklist |
| Chambers of commerce | Local owner introductions | Explore member education session |
| Telephony consultants | Understand call routing/provider limits | Map sandbox options |
| CRM implementation consultants | Complementary lead-management expertise | Compare handoff and delivery boundaries |
| Small-business coaches | Advise owners on operations | Offer a non-exclusive educational resource |

This is a category list, not ten validated named prospects. A sourced partner shortlist remains separate research work.

## CRM stages and required fields

Stages: New → Researching → Qualified → Discovery scheduled → Discovery completed → Demo scheduled → Demo completed → Proposal/pilot scoped → Won / Lost / Nurture / Suppressed.

Required fields: business name, website, industry, location, source URL, research date, public business contact channel, fit rationale, publicly confirmed buyer role, stage, owner, next action/date, last contact date, channel, applicable consent/legal basis, opt-out/suppression status, and outcome.

Deduplicate by normalized business domain plus business name/location. Do not guess email addresses or personal phone numbers. A CRM record is not consent.

## Compliance and stop rules

- Commercial email must use accurate sender and subject information, required postal address, functioning unsubscribe, and timely suppression.
- No cold SMS marketing. Use SMS only with documented channel-appropriate consent and provider/legal review.
- Respect do-not-contact requests across channels; never re-import suppressed contacts.
- No fake personalization, deceptive urgency, unsupported performance claims, or fabricated case studies.
- Contact form submission does not automatically authorize unrelated promotional SMS.
- Transactional messages and promotional nurture are separate purposes with separate controls.
- Verify current federal, state, carrier, and platform requirements before launch. This is an operational checklist, not legal advice.
